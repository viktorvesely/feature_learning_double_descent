\section{Introduction}

Model-wise double descent challenges the expectation that increasing capacity beyond interpolation necessarily harms generalization. As models grow, validation error can decrease, peak near interpolation, and decrease again despite fitting the training noise. Two streams of literature seek to explain this curve: frozen-kernel analyses of the instability of interpolation, and empirical studies of deep networks that learn their representations. Their common target suggests a common mechanism; we show that the same curve shape can arise from two distinct mechanisms.

Frozen-kernel explanations locate the peak in the amplification of noise by weakly constrained feature directions. Near interpolation, fitting residual errors along these directions requires large coefficients that can transfer poorly to unseen data; greater capacity can reduce this sensitivity \citep{bach2023highdimensionalanalysisdoubledescent,schaeffer2023doubledescentdemystifiedidentifying}. A finite-width extension allows features to change but assumes that the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of their gradient covariance remains comparable to its initial value, retaining a spectral link to initialization \citep{singh2022phenomenologydoubledescentfinitewidth}. More recently, a theory accommodating evolving kernels also recovers double descent through a frozen-kernel specialization \citep{litman2026theorygeneralizationdeeplearning}. These explanations describe how noise is fitted within an available representation.

Empirical deep-learning studies instead observe double descent as the representation itself changes with training and model width. Analyses of learned feature neighborhoods associate the second descent with improved separation of corrupted labels from class structure \citep{gu2024unravelingenigmadoubledescent}. This raises a complementary question about the peak: can learning the representation itself create the deterioration in generalization? Resolving this question distinguishes a peak caused by feature formation from the interpolation instability described by frozen-kernel theory.

Learning features that facilitate noise fitting provides a candidate mechanism for this empirical peak. The closest work characterizes a transition from signal learning to memorization of sample-specific noise in a two-layer convolutional network \citep{pmlr-v267-han25c}. Its focus is the evolution of signal and noise learning during optimization. Our focus is how such feature changes generate a nonmonotonic generalization curve across model widths, which samples induce them, and whether suppressing their feature-learning contribution reduces the peak while preserving their fit. Related observations of clean- and noisy-label learning in epoch-wise double descent further motivate distinguishing the progress of noise fitting from its consequences across model sizes \citep{TomokiKUBO2025}.

We identify two distinct mechanisms of model-wise double descent within the same task and architecture. On MNIST and CIFAR-10 with label corruption, \emph{feature-learning double descent} (FLDD) arises earlier as learned features accelerate noise fitting, while \emph{frozen-kernel double descent} (FKDD) emerges at longer horizons through slowly learned, weakly constrained directions. Matched nonlinear and frozen-feature models distinguish the mechanisms. Freezing trained features and evaluating their finite-time kernel dynamics reveals a later FKDD peak alongside the existing FLDD peak (Figure~\ref{fig:double-dd-showcase}). This connects the two literature streams through distinct mechanisms that can coexist, rather than treating their explanations as interchangeable.

We test the feature-learning mechanism by intervening on the samples that drive noise-fitting feature changes. We attribute these changes to individual examples, which may be clean or noisy, and selectively disable their contribution to feature learning. The selected examples remain trainable through a readout, preserving the ability to fit them. The resulting reduction in the peak links FLDD to the formation of noise-fitting features and provides a targeted experimental test of the proposed mechanism.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our contributions are twofold:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(C1) Identifying two distinct mechanisms of model-wise double descent.} We distinguish FLDD from FKDD through matched feature-learning ablations and demonstrate their coexistence through frozen-kernel dynamics evaluated at trained checkpoints.

    \item \textbf{(C2) Identifying and intervening on the feature-learning mechanism.} We derive sample-level scores for feature changes that facilitate noise fitting and reduce the FLDD peak by selectively disabling these changes while preserving the ability to fit the selected samples.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

We consider a supervised learning setting with a training dataset $\sS = \{\vx_s, \vy_s\}_{l=1}^{S}$ of $S$ i.i.d.\ samples, where $\vx \in \sR^{d_x}$ denotes the input and $\vy \in \sR^{d_y}$ the corresponding real valued output for regression and $\vy \in \{0, 1\}^{d_y}$ the one-hot vector over $d_y$ classes for classification. Analogously, $\sQ = \{\vx_q, \vy_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}$ defines the testing dataset with $Q$ samples.

We define $\vtheta_{n,p} \in \sR^{p}$ to be $p$ parameters of a neural network $f_{\vtheta}$ trained for $n$ epochs. We train the parameters by minimizing the training loss $\mathcal{L}_{\sS}$ which is instantiated to MSE for regression tasks and CE for classification tasks. Test loss $\mathcal{L}_{\sQ}$ is used to measure the final performance.

We train our models with the plain stochastic gradient descent for random batches $\sB \subset \sS$ and a constant learning rate $\eta$, yielding the update rule $\theta_{t + 1} = \theta{t} + \eta \nabla_{\theta_t} \batchloss(\theta_t)$.

To induce double descent in classification tasks, we introduce label noise at a rate $\xi \in [0,1]$. Before training, we select a fraction $\xi$ of the training samples uniformly at random and replace each selected label with a label drawn uniformly from the remaining $d_y - 1$ classes for classification or drawn uniformly from other samples otputs for regression. Both the selected samples and their corrupted labels remain fixed throughout training, while the test labels remain unchanged.

\subsection{Taylor approximation of the SGD learning dynamics}


\paragraph{Definitions}
We examine the change in loss induced by an SGD update on a training batch $\sB \subset \sS$. To distinguish the contribution of the current tangent features from that of their evolution, we express the update in terms of output gradients and parameter Jacobians. Unless stated otherwise, quantities below are evaluated at the current parameters $\vtheta_t$, with this dependence suppressed for readability.

\begin{align*}
    \vb \coloneqq (f_\vtheta(\vx_1): \ldots : f_\vtheta(\vx_{|\sB|})), \quad \vx_i \in \sB  \quad & \text{Batch outputs' concatenation;} \\
    \vg_\sB \coloneqq \nabla_\vb \batchloss \quad & \text{Output training gradient;}\\
    \mJ_{\sB} \coloneqq \nabla_\theta \vb \quad &  \text{Jacobian of batch otuputs;}\\
    \vp_\sB \coloneqq  \mJ^\top_{\sB} \vg_\sB  \quad & \text{Parameter training gradient for the batch.}\\
    K_{\sX \sY} \coloneqq  \mJ_{\sX} \mJ^\top_{\sY}  \quad & \text{Cross-kernel matrix between set $\sX$ and set $\sY$}
\end{align*}


\paragraph{Local loss dynamics}
For the update $\vtheta_{t+1} = \vtheta_t - \eta\vp_\sB$, we define the batch-loss change as $\Delta\batchloss \coloneqq \batchloss(\vtheta_{t+1}) - \batchloss(\vtheta_t)$. We approximate this change by a second-order Taylor expansion along the straight parameter-space path of the update. The update direction is held fixed along this path; the dots denote directional derivatives evaluated at $\vtheta_t$. The resulting approximation separates the first-order change from two contributions to the second-order change:
\begin{align}
    \Delta \batchloss &\approx \Delta t \dot{\batchloss} + \frac{(\Delta t)^2}{2} \ddot{\batchloss} \label{eq:taylor-approx} \\
    \dot{\batchloss} &= -\vg^\top_\sB \mJ_{\sB} \mJ^\top_{\sB} \vg_\sB \\
    \ddot{\batchloss} &= \underbrace{\vp_\sB^\top \mJ^\top_\sB (\nabla^2_f \batchloss) \mJ_\sB \vp_\sB }_{\text{Loss curvature}} + \underbrace{\vp_\sB^\top \left( \sum_{i=1}^{|\sB|d_y} (\vg_\sB)_i \nabla^2_\vtheta b_i \right) \vp_\sB }_{\text{Feature-learning curvature}}
\end{align}
Here the step length equals the learning rate, $\Delta t = \eta$. The first-order term describes descent through the current tangent features. At second order, the loss-curvature term accounts for curvature of the loss with respect to the model outputs, whereas the feature-learning curvature term depends on the second derivatives of those outputs with respect to the parameters. Second order is thus the lowest order at which output curvature appears explicitly in this local expansion. Appendix~\ref{ap:taylor-derivation} provides the derivation, and Appendix~\ref{ap:matrix-free} provides the implemented matrix-free versions of the presented terms. 

Equation~\ref{eq:taylor-approx} is a local approximation. Its accuracy over a finite SGD step depends on how the directional derivatives vary along the update path; a second-order expansion at the starting point need not adequately capture the full loss change. This motivates integration along the update path, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{ap:taylor-integrated}.

For a frozen kernel models the feature-learning curvature term vanishes, explicitly identifying the difference between feature learning and frozen kernel models. The feature learning term misses kernel changes that are locally independent to the loss changes.  To complement this directional quantity, we measure changes in the training kernel over the course of optimization. We track the training kernel $K_t=J_{\sS}(\vtheta_t)J_{\sS}(\vtheta_t)^\top$ using two relative Frobenius-norm metrics: its drift from initialization and its change since the previous measurement $t^-$,
\begin{align}
    D_t &= \frac{\|K_t-K_0\|_F}{\|K_0\|_F}, &
    S_t &= \frac{\|K_t-K_{t^-}\|_F}{\|K_0\|_F}.
\end{align}
The first metric measures cumulative drift from initialization, while the second measures change between successive measurements.


\paragraph{Transfer to validation loss}
The effect of a training update on validation loss is determined, to first order, by the alignment between the batch and validation parameter gradients. Let $\vg_\sQ$ denote the gradient of $\genloss$ with respect to the concatenated validation outputs, and let $\mJ_\sQ$ denote their parameter Jacobian. Along the same SGD update path, the chain rule gives 
\begin{align}
    \dot{\genloss}
    &= -\bigl(\mJ_\sQ^\top\vg_\sQ\bigr)^\top\vp_\sB
     = -\vg_\sQ^\top K_{\sQ\sB}\vg_\sB.
    \label{eq:validation-transfer}
\end{align}
To obtain interpretable scalar metrics for each training sample's contribution to validation learning, we separate its output-gradient magnitude from its transfer to validation. Let $\vg_\sB^{(i)}$ be its block of the batch output gradient and $(\mJ_\sB^\top)^{(i)}$ the corresponding block of the transposed Jacobian. Writing $\hat{\vg}_\sB^{(i)} = \vg_\sB^{(i)}/\|\vg_\sB^{(i)}\|_2$, with $\hat{\vg}_\sB^{(i)}=0$ when the gradient vanishes, we define the \emph{Train push} $P_i$ and \emph{Transfer} $T_i$ through the decomposition
\begin{align}
    \dot{\genloss}
    &= -\sum_{i=1}^{|\sB|}
    \underbrace{\|\vg_\sB^{(i)}\|_2}_{P_i\;\text{(Train push)}}
    \underbrace{
        \vg_\sQ^\top\mJ_\sQ
        (\mJ_\sB^\top)^{(i)}
        \hat{\vg}_\sB^{(i)}
    }_{T_i\;\text{(Transfer)}} = -\sum_{i=1}^{|\sB|} P_i T_i.
    \label{eq:per-sample-transfer}
\end{align}
Train push $P_i$ measures the magnitude of the sample's output gradient, while Transfer $T_i$ measures the alignment of the corresponding parameter direction with the validation gradient. Positive $T_i$ therefore contributes to a first-order decrease in validation loss, and negative $T_i$ contributes to an increase. This decomposition separates how strongly a sample drives the update from how beneficial that direction is for validation performance.

\subsection{Kernel regression}

We use finite-time kernel regression to evaluate how validation performance evolves when the current features remain fixed. At a chosen checkpoint, we freeze their parameter Jacobian, obtaining an affine model of subsequent parameter changes. We evaluate the evolution of this model under full-batch gradient descent on a squared-error objective. This allows us to explore long training horizons without explicitly simulating every update, including the slow learning of kernel modes with small eigenvalues. Computational details are given in Appendix~\ref{ap:matrix-free}.

We apply this continuation both at initialization and after training, across model widths and random seeds. Freezing at initialization isolates frozen-kernel double descent (FKDD), while freezing a trained network that already exhibits feature-learning double descent (FLDD) allows us to examine whether a later FKDD develops alongside it. These continuations separate the effects of continued fitting with fixed features from those of further feature learning.

For regression, we linearize the model outputs and use mean squared error. For classification, we linearize the softmax probabilities and fit the labels with Brier loss, reporting validation error averaged over output coordinates. The classification continuation therefore describes squared-error learning in the affine probability model, rather than continued cross-entropy training of the original network.

\subsection{Frozen Kernel Double Descent mechanism}

The mechanisms underlying frozen-kernel double descent have been studied extensively. Here, we follow the three-factor account of \citet{schaeffer2023doubledescentdemystifiedidentifying}, applied to the frozen Jacobian as the feature matrix. The mechanism combines small nonzero eigenvalues of the empirical tangent kernel, residual error from noise or model misspecification aligned with the corresponding training modes, and validation features that amplify the resulting estimation error. Fitting this residual along weakly constrained directions requires large parameter coefficients, which can produce large prediction errors on validation samples. We refer to double descent arising through this mechanism as frozen-kernel double descent (FKDD).

This account describes FKDD through spectral modes, whereas our proposed feature-learning double descent (FLDD) mechanism focuses on how individual training samples drive changes in the features. Ridge regularization mitigates the FKDD mechanism by suppressing the amplification associated with small eigenvalues.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Specification of the double descent curve}
\label{sec:dd-specification}

Model-wise double descent traces a reversal in the benefit of increasing
model capacity: validation loss initially falls, rises to a peak, and
then falls again as the model grows wider. To study how learning changes
across this curve, we summarize its geometry at a fixed training horizon
$T$ by four ordered widths,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_T \coloneqq
    (w_{\mathrm{s}},w_-,w_\star,w_+),
    \qquad w_{\mathrm{s}}<w_-<w_\star<w_+.
    \label{eq:dd-landmarks}
\end{equation}
Here $w_{\mathrm{s}}$ is the smallest width considered, $w_-$ marks the
validation-loss minimum before the peak, $w_\star$ the peak itself, and
$w_+$ the minimum reached beyond it within the explored range.
Together, these landmarks place the deterioration in generalization
between two reference models with lower validation loss, while retaining
the starting point of the width sweep.

This four-point description gives us a common language for comparing 
learning dynamics across the curve and asking how an intervention changes
its shape. We identify the landmarks from the final validation losses,
requiring a clearly distinguished peak with lower loss on either side.
The numerical identification procedure is given in
Appendix~\ref{ap:dd-identification}.

\subsection{Feature-Learning Double Descent Mechanism}
\label{sec:fldd-mechanism}

We hypothesize that feature learning can give rise to double descent by
creating representations that make label noise easier to fit. The proposed
mechanism involves three conditions: (i) updates induced by some training
samples create features that facilitate noise fitting; (ii) the network
has sufficient capacity to fit the noise and does so rapidly once these
features emerge; and (iii) fitting the noisy samples transfers poorly to
validation data. We refer to double descent associated with this mechanism
as \emph{feature-learning double descent} (FLDD).

\paragraph{Feature changes that facilitate noise fitting.}
To formalize condition~(i), let $\sL\subseteq\sS$ denote the noisy samples
in the training dataset. Along the straight-line path of an SGD
update, $\dot{\vtheta}=-\vp_\sB$, the quantity
$\vg_\sL^\top J_\sL\vp_\sB$ measures the instantaneous rate of decrease
of the noisy-sample loss. Its change separates into contributions from
the output gradients and the features:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\vg_\sL^\top J_\sL\vp_\sB\bigr)
    = \underbrace{\dot{\vg}_\sL^\top J_\sL\vp_\sB}_{\text{output-gradient change}}
    + \underbrace{\vg_\sL^\top\dot{J}_\sL\vp_\sB}_{\text{feature change}}
    + \underbrace{\vg_\sL^\top J_\sL\dot{\vp}_\sB}_{=\,0\;\text{along a fixed SGD step}}.
\end{equation}
Dots denote time derivatives along the straight-line SGD step, evaluated
at its start. The update direction $\vp_\sB$ is fixed during this step,
so $\dot{\vp}_\sB=0$.

We isolate the feature contribution and attribute it to the samples
providing the update. Writing $\vp_i=J_i^\top\vg_i$ and
$\vp_\sB=\sum_{i\in\sB}\vp_i$, define
\begin{align}
    H_{\mathrm{model},\sL}
    &\coloneqq \sum_{j\in\sL}\sum_{a=1}^{d_y}
        (\vg_j)_a\nabla_\vtheta^2 f_{\vtheta,a}(\vx_j), \\
    \dot{J}_\sL^\top\vg_\sL
    &= -H_{\mathrm{model},\sL}\vp_\sB, \\
    \vg_\sL^\top\dot{J}_\sL\vp_\sB
    &= \sum_{i\in\sB}l_i,
    \qquad l_i\coloneqq-\vp_i^\top H_{\mathrm{model},\sL}\vp_\sB.
    \label{eq:fldd-sample-contribution}
\end{align}
By symmetry of the Hessian, $l_i$ measures how the feature change induced
by sample $i$ affects the rate of noise fitting along the batch update.
The contributing sample may be clean or noisy; a positive $l_i$ indicates
that its feature change facilitates noise fitting. The output gradients
retain the original batch-loss normalization.

We accumulate these contributions over training to obtain a score for
each sample:
\begin{equation}
    C_i(T)=\sum_{\substack{t\text{ before horizon }T\\i\in\sB_t}}
        \frac{(\Delta t_t)^2}{2}\,l_{i,t}.
    \label{eq:fldd-cumulative-contribution}
\end{equation}
Here $t$ indexes SGD updates, $i$ identifies the same dataset sample
across batches, and $l_{i,t}$ is evaluated at the start of the update.
The factor $(\Delta t_t)^2/2$ weights the contribution according to the
second-order expansion of the loss decrease over that step.

\paragraph{Design for disabling feature learning per sample} \label{sec:disable-sample-feature-learning}

In order to test our mechanism which attributes the FLDD to a feature learning mechanism of a specific group of samples, we need to come up with a specific mechanism that allows allows only subset of samples to do feture learning. Precisely, we seek a design that prevents a subset of training examples $\sF \subset \sS$ (lets call them frozen samples) not to infleunce the feature kernel of the neural network $f_\vtheta$.  Most elegant way is to use Equation \ref{eq:frozen-kernel} to implement a two pathway model where both the $f_\vtheta(\vx)$ term si allowed to change as well as the $\vw$ in the linear term $(\nabla_{\vtheta_0} f_{\vtheta_0}) \vw$. However, such model is unstable and does not converge (more about this and more designs are shown in the Appendix \ref{ap:disable-sample-feature-learning}).

We have settled on a two pathway architecture design where the the frozen samples $\sF$ can only update the linear readout weights $\mW \in \sR^{(\lvert \vh(\vx) \rvert + \lvert \vx \rvert ) \times d_y}$ from the concatenation of all the network's hidden activations $\vh(\vx)$ and the input itself $\vx$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:fl-ablatoion-mixed}
    \bar{f}_{\vtheta, \mW} (\vx) = f_\vtheta(\vx) + (\vh(\vx) :  \vx) \mW,
\end{equation}
where the gradient for parameters $\vtheta$ is stopped for the frozen samples $\vx \in \sF$. This allows very precisely validate our mechanism.



\paragraph{Testing the mechanism.}
We do not claim that conditions~(i)--(iii) are sufficient for FLDD to arise.
Its emergence also depends on the noise fraction, optimizer, batch size,
and learning rate.
% TODO: Add citations for these dependencies.
Our aim is to identify the feature-learning contribution to the peak.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:fldd-intervention} tests this contribution by disabling
feature learning for samples with the largest cumulative scores, while
preserving the model's ability to fit them through the intervention in
Section~\ref{sec:disable-sample-feature-learning}. A reduction in the
validation-loss peak would support the proposed mechanism.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{FLDD intervention algorithm}
\label{alg:fldd-intervention}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Width sweep $\mathcal{W}$, training horizon $T$, intervention size $n$
\Ensure Baseline and intervened validation-loss curves
\For{each width $w\in\mathcal{W}$}
    \State Train the baseline model to horizon $T$, accumulating $C_i(T)$ for each sample
    \State Select the $n$ samples with the largest $C_i(T)$
    \State Retrain from the same initialization, disabling feature learning for the selected samples
    \State Keep the data order, training horizon, and all other training settings fixed
    \State Record the baseline and intervened validation losses
\EndFor
\State Compare the two curves across widths, assessing the reduction in the double-descent peak
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Identification of the double descent landmarks}
\label{ap:dd-identification}

We extract the four landmarks from the validation-loss curve at a fixed
training horizon, using Algorithm~\ref{alg:dd-identification}. Let
$\ell_i$ denote the final validation loss at width $w_i$, averaged over
seeds when applicable. Smoothing suppresses small fluctuations so that
the selected peak reflects the broader shape of the curve.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Four-point identification of double descent}
\label{alg:dd-identification}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Validation losses $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_m$ at widths $w_1<\cdots<w_m$, $m\geq4$
\Ensure Landmarks $\mathcal{D}_T$, if a sufficiently distinct peak is present
\State Smooth $\ell$ with a Gaussian filter to obtain $\widetilde\ell$
\State Set the contrast threshold $\delta\gets 0.1\lvert\widetilde\ell_1\rvert$
\State Identify the most prominent peak $i_\star$ of $\widetilde\ell$ with prominence at least $\delta$
\If{$i_\star$ exists, is dominant, and $3\leq i_\star\leq m-1$}
    \State Select $i_-$ minimizing $\ell_i$ over $1<i<i_\star$
    \State Select $i_+$ minimizing $\widetilde\ell_i$ over $i_\star<i\leq m$
    \If{the smoothed peak exceeds both selected minima by at least $\delta$}
        \State \Return $\mathcal{D}_T=(w_1,w_{i_-},w_{i_\star},w_{i_+})$
    \EndIf
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We use Gaussian bandwidth $\sigma=\min\{3,\max\{0.1,m/30\}\}$,
measured in grid positions, with constant extension at the boundaries.
A peak's prominence is its height above the higher of the two surrounding
valley floors, each delimited by a higher peak or the boundary of the
curve. A peak is \emph{dominant} if every other peak with prominence at
least $\delta$ has less than half its prominence. Ties between minima
are resolved in favor of the smaller width. The pre-peak minimum uses
the observed losses, while the post-peak minimum and both contrast
comparisons use the smoothed curve. Curves that do not meet the selection
criteria remain unidentified.

This criterion permits local fluctuations between landmarks and does not
separately require a decrease from the starting width to the pre-peak
minimum. The selected peak describes the validation-loss curve; relating
it to an interpolation threshold requires training-loss evidence.

\subsection{Derivation of the 2nd order taylor loss changes}
\label{ap:taylor-derivation}

\begin{align}
    \genloss(t)
    &= \genloss(\theta(t))
    && \text{Validation loss} \\
    \theta_t(\tau)
    &= \theta(t) - \tau \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_S(\theta(t))
    && \text{Linear interpolation of one SGD step} \\
    \genloss(\theta_t(\Delta t))
    &= \genloss(\theta_t(0))
    + \int_0^{\Delta t}
    \dot{\genloss}(\theta_t(\tau)) \, d\tau
    && \text{Approximate with Taylor series} \\
    \genloss(\theta_t(\Delta t))
    &\approx \genloss(\theta_t(0))
    + \Delta t
    \left.\dot{\genloss}\right|_{\tau=0}
    + \frac{1}{2} \Delta t^2

\subsection{Matrix free formulations}

\paragraph{Kernel regression}
We evaluate the finite-time gradient-descent solution through a spectral filter, approximated using a matrix-free Golub--Kahan reduction of the loss-normalized Jacobian. Jacobian-vector and vector-Jacobian products construct the reduced problem without explicitly forming the Jacobian or kernel matrices. We apply the finite-time filter to the singular values of the reduced problem without ridge regularization.


With respect to the kernel changes:
We estimate both quantities using the same fixed Rademacher probes $\vr_1,\ldots,\vr_R$:
\begin{align}
    \widehat D_t
    &= \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|(K_t-K_0)\vr_r\|_2^2}
                        {\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|K_0\vr_r\|_2^2}}, &
    \widehat S_t
    &= \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|(K_t-K_{t^-})\vr_r\|_2^2}
                        {\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|K_0\vr_r\|_2^2}}.
\end{align}
